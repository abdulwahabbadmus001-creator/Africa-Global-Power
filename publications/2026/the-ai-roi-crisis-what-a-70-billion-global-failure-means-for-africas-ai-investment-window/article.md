## Executive Summary

Global enterprises have spent over $70 billion on generative AI pilots since 2023, and the evidence on returns is now impossible to ignore. The overwhelming majority of that investment has produced no measurable financial return. This brief argues that the failure is not technological. It is a human capital failure dressed up as a technology story, and it carries an urgent warning for Africa specifically, because the continent is entering its own AI procurement cycle right as the evidence of what happens without proper sequencing becomes undeniable.

Between 2023 and 2025, MIT found that 95 percent of a surveyed cohort of American enterprises using generative AI reported zero measurable return on investment. S&P Global found that AI initiative abandonment jumped from 17 percent in 2024 to 42 percent in 2025. RAND found that AI projects fail at roughly twice the rate of standard technology projects. None of this is a technology problem. It is what happens when organisations buy tools and defund the people meant to operate them.

Africa is not exempt from this risk, and in some respects is more exposed to it. The African Union's Continental AI Strategy, endorsed in July 2024, and Nigeria's National AI Strategy, published in September 2025, both set ambitious continental and national AI adoption targets. Both also face the same structural gap already responsible for enterprise failure elsewhere: chronic underinvestment in the human capital required to operate, govern and interrogate AI systems responsibly. Nigeria's own government data places national digital literacy at 50 percent. This brief sets out what African governments, institutions and enterprises should do differently, while the window to sequence this correctly is still open.



## Research Brief


**Research question:** Why is enterprise AI investment failing to generate returns globally, and what must African governments and institutions do differently to avoid repeating the same failure as they begin their own large-scale AI adoption?


**Why the topic matters:** African governments and development partners are actively planning large AI investments right now, guided by the AU Continental AI Strategy and national strategies such as Nigeria's NAIS. The evidence on what causes AI investment to fail is already available. The question is whether African policymakers act on it before procurement begins, or after.


**Current situation:** Independent research from MIT, S&P Global, RAND, Deloitte, McKinsey and Goldman Sachs converges on one finding: most enterprise AI investment is not generating measurable return, and the primary cause is under-investment in human capital rather than the technology itself. Africa-specific evidence shows AU strategy implementation concentrated in a handful of countries, and Nigeria's own strategy openly documents skills, infrastructure and literacy gaps.


**Competing perspectives:** Technology vendors and some government communications continue to frame AI adoption primarily as an infrastructure and procurement question. The evidence reviewed here, including Africa's own strategy documents, points instead to human capital as the binding constraint.


**Evidence gaps:** There is limited Africa-specific empirical data on AI ROI at enterprise or government level. This brief relies on structural indicators, digital literacy, R&D spend, strategy implementation data, rather than direct ROI measurement, because that measurement does not yet exist for the continent.


**Key findings:** The AI ROI crisis is a human capital crisis. Africa's own strategy documents already acknowledge the structural gaps responsible for that crisis elsewhere. The remaining question is one of political will and budget sequencing, not awareness.

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence has become the most capital-intensive corporate bet since the early internet buildout. Enterprises spent over $70 billion on generative AI pilots between 2023 and 2025. Big Tech alone is on course for $325 billion in AI-related capital expenditure in 2025, up from $223 billion the year before. Almost none of it is generating a measurable return.

That failure is not a story confined to Silicon Valley and European boardrooms. It is a direct warning for Africa, because the continent is now entering its own large-scale AI adoption cycle, guided by the African Union's Continental AI Strategy and a wave of national strategies including Nigeria's. The evidence on what causes enterprise AI investment to fail already exists. What is not yet decided is whether African governments and institutions act on that evidence before they commit public funds, or after.



## Background: The Scale of a Global Failure

MIT's 2025 report, The GenAI Divide, found that 95 percent of a surveyed cohort of American enterprises using generative AI reported zero measurable return on investment. That figure is specific to one American cohort and should not be read as a universal constant, but the directional finding, that most enterprises are not generating returns commensurate with spend, holds across every major study reviewed for this brief.

S&P Global Market Intelligence's 2025 survey of over 1,000 enterprises found that AI initiative abandonment rose from 17 percent in 2024 to 42 percent in 2025, with the average organisation scrapping 46 percent of its AI proof-of-concept projects before they ever reached production.

RAND Corporation's analysis found that AI projects fail at roughly twice the rate of standard technology projects.

Goldman Sachs raised the alarm earliest. Its 2024 report, Gen AI: Too Much Spend, Too Little Benefit?, questioned whether over $1 trillion in planned AI investment would deliver commensurate returns. Two years on, the firm's head of global equity research said he remained only more convinced the returns were not materialising.



## Analysis: Four Root Causes

Four failures explain most of this gap.

First, organisations are funding AI as a replacement for skilled professionals rather than as a tool that requires them. Current AI systems perform high-speed pattern recognition and task automation well. They do not exercise independent judgement or carry institutional responsibility. Enterprises that cut expert headcount while increasing AI tooling budgets, a pattern documented across Amazon, Microsoft, IBM and Salesforce, are removing the exact capability that would make the tool work.

Second, human development is being defunded at the moment it matters most. Informatica's 2025 CDO Insights survey found data readiness at 43 percent, technical maturity at 43 percent and skills shortage at 35 percent as the leading obstacles to AI success. These are human capital problems, not technology problems, and no software purchase resolves them.

Third, most pilots are never designed to scale. They are built under controlled conditions that ignore organisational complexity, regulatory constraint and legacy system integration, and they stall permanently without expert programme managers who understand both the technology and the institution deploying it.

Fourth, measurement frameworks built for a different era miss AI's real value. Process efficiency, error reduction and faster decision cycles rarely appear in short-cycle financial ROI metrics, so programmes that are genuinely improving operations get cancelled because the improvement is invisible to the metric being used.

Google's own AI Overviews launch in May 2024 illustrates the cost of skipping human oversight. Within days, the feature was producing factually incorrect and misleading results, and Google was forced to scale it back. No AI system should face users in a high-stakes context without a human quality layer between the model and the output.



## Analysis: Africa's Investment Window

This is where the global evidence becomes a direct African policy question.

The African Union's Continental AI Strategy, endorsed at the 45th Ordinary Session in Accra in July 2024, commits all 55 member states to an Africa-centric, development-focused approach to AI adoption aligned with Agenda 2063. Implementation runs in two phases: governance structures and national strategies through 2025 and 2026, core project execution from 2028 following a 2027 review.

The ambition is continental. The resourcing is not. An 18-month implementation review published via ResearchGate in October 2025 found that 83 percent of AI funding across the continent was concentrated in just four countries, against a continental need the strategy itself puts at $500 billion. Private sector mobilisation remains minimal relative to that figure.

Nigeria's National Artificial Intelligence Strategy, published in September 2025, sets a five-year vision to 2029 built on economic growth, social inclusion and technological advancement, with a target of upskilling 100,000 professionals by 2026. The strategy is candid about the gap it is working from. Nigeria spends roughly 0.2 percent of GDP on research and development against a global average of 2.2 percent, ranks 103rd on the Oxford Insights AI Readiness Index, and continues to lose skilled professionals to brain drain.

NITDA's own July 2025 data places national digital literacy at 50 percent, with only 39 percent of Nigerians able to operate a laptop or tablet. The agency's target of 95 percent literacy by 2030 is necessary and realistic on paper. It is also a direct signal that the workforce required to govern, operate and audit AI systems at national scale does not yet exist at the scale the strategy assumes.

In April 2025, 49 African states signed the Kigali Declaration on Artificial Intelligence, a collective commitment to AI adoption across the continent. That commitment is not in question. What remains open is whether it will be matched by an equally deliberate commitment to the human capital that makes AI adoption succeed rather than fail, at a scale documented across every enterprise study in this brief.



## Challenges and Limitations

Africa-specific empirical data on AI ROI is limited. This brief relies on structural indicators, digital literacy, R&D spend, strategy implementation figures, rather than direct measurement of return, because that measurement does not yet exist at scale for African governments or enterprises. The global enterprise data reviewed here comes primarily from American and European organisations, and Africa's institutional context, cost structures and adoption stage differ in ways that may change how directly these findings transfer. The directional conclusion, that human capital investment determines AI return more than technology selection, is nonetheless consistent enough across contexts to treat as a reliable planning assumption rather than a settled measurement.



## Opportunities and Recommendations

**For Governments**

- Audit national digital literacy before AI procurement begins, not alongside it.

- Allocate at least 50 percent of AI budgets to data infrastructure and human capital development rather than tooling.

- Require a human oversight layer for every high-stakes public-sector AI deployment.

- Establish independent AI audit and oversight mechanisms before large-scale rollout, not after.


**For African Institutions**

The African Union should treat the 83 percent funding concentration finding as an implementation emergency rather than a footnote, and prioritise resource mobilisation toward the 51 member states currently outside the four-country concentration.


**For Private Sector** 

Redesign workflows before selecting AI tools. McKinsey's 2025 data shows organisations that redesign end-to-end processes first are twice as likely to report significant financial returns. Plan for a two to four year ROI horizon, the realistic payback window Deloitte's 2025 research identifies, rather than cancelling programmes on a quarterly return expectation.


**For International Partners**

Development finance aimed at African AI adoption should fund data infrastructure and workforce development at the same scale as compute and platform procurement. Funding the tool without funding the operator repeats the exact error this brief documents.


**For Researchers**

Africa-specific empirical research on AI ROI, currently a significant evidence gap, would allow future policy to move from structural proxy indicators to direct measurement.



## What This Means for You


- If you are a policymaker, treat digital literacy and workforce readiness as a prerequisite for AI procurement, not a parallel workstream you get to later.

- If you run a business, redesign your workflow and identify your data readiness gaps before you sign a platform contract, not after.

- If you are an investor or development partner, ask any AI-focused proposal what share of its budget goes to people rather than tools, and treat a low answer as a red flag.

- If you are a student or early-career professional in AI or data, the skills gap documented in this brief is not bad news for you. It is the clearest signal available that Africa's AI economy needs exactly what you are building toward.



## Conclusion


The AI ROI crisis is a human capital crisis wearing a technology label. The conclusion holds across every enterprise study reviewed in this brief, and it holds with even greater force for Africa, where the workforce and infrastructure gaps are wider and the public resources available to absorb a procurement mistake are smaller.

The investment cycles that locked American and European enterprises into tool-first, people-second strategies are not yet complete on the continent. Africa's own strategy documents already identify the gaps. What remains is the political will to fund people before platforms, while that choice is still available.
