## Executive Summary

In April 2026, South Africa's Cabinet approved a draft National AI Policy meant to position the country as a continental leader in AI regulation. Weeks later, journalists found that at least six of the document's 67 academic citations were fabrications generated by AI, referencing articles that had never been published. Communications Minister Solly Malatsi withdrew the policy in full, calling the failure a compromise of its integrity and credibility. The government has since pushed its AI governance timeline back roughly nine months, with a revised draft due to Cabinet in November 2026 and public comment reopening in January 2027. 

This publication treats the episode as more than a single country's embarrassment. It is a case study in institutional readiness, a warning about unverified AI use inside government, and a preview of a risk every African state now drafting AI policy will face. South Africa was not alone in trying to move fast on AI regulation. Egypt, Rwanda, Mauritius and Zambia had already adopted national AI policies, and South Africa's draft was meant to align with the African Union's Continental AI Strategy timeline. The question this analysis answers is what safeguards would have prevented this failure, and what every African government, regulator and drafting team should build before publishing the next policy document. 


## Research Brief

**Research question:** What does South Africa's withdrawn AI policy reveal about institutional readiness for AI governance across Africa, and what safeguards can prevent a repeat?


**Why the topic matters:** Africa is at an early and formative stage of AI regulation. Mistakes made now set precedent, shape public trust and influence how seriously international partners take African AI governance capacity.


**Historical context:** The policy process began with a National AI Summit convened in April 2024, and the final draft was published in the Government Gazette on 10 April 2026 under the constitutional provision allowing the executive to develop national policy. The draft extended South Africa's initial AI policy framework and had already cleared Cabinet before publication. 


**Current situation:** A revised policy remains on track for Cabinet submission in November 2026, with January 2027 as a fallback date. An independent panel of experts has been appointed to review the withdrawn draft, remove flawed sections and replace fabricated citations with verified sources. Two officials have been placed on precautionary suspension pending investigation. 


**Major stakeholders:** The Department of Communications and Digital Technologies, Parliament's portfolio committee on communications, the Academy of Science of South Africa, South African academic journal editors, and by extension every African government currently drafting comparable frameworks.


**Competing perspectives:** Government framing presents the withdrawal as evidence of functioning oversight. Opposition figures and civil society have called the failure embarrassing and damning, demanding full transparency and accountability. The Academy of Science of South Africa has taken a more constructive line, calling the episode an important opportunity to rebuild public trust in AI oversight despite the concerns it raised about the integrity of the policy process. 


**Opportunities:** A rebuilt policy grounded in verified evidence, a template of verification protocols other African states can adopt before this happens to them, and a stronger case for independent technical review of any government document produced with AI assistance.


**Risks:** Delayed regulation leaves South Africa without an AI governance framework at a time when AI is already embedded in hiring, lending and public administration decisions. Reputational damage may also affect how seriously South Africa's technical capacity is viewed by international partners and investors.


**Evidence gaps:** Full details of the internal review process, and the specific findings of the seven-member expert panel, are not yet public.


**Key findings:** The scandal was preventable through basic citation verification. It was caught by journalists, not government processes. And the underlying policy ambition, a risk-based framework modelled on the EU AI Act, remains sound even though the execution failed.

## Full Publication

**Introduction**

South Africa set out to become a continental leader in AI regulation. Instead, it became a cautionary tale. In April 2026, the Department of Communications and Digital Technologies published a draft National AI Policy that had already cleared Cabinet twice. Weeks later, that same policy was withdrawn in full after journalists discovered that its reference list contained citations to academic papers that do not exist.

The irony is obvious. A policy meant to govern artificial intelligence appears to have been partly written by it, without anyone checking the output. But the deeper story is not about irony. It is about institutional capacity, and about what happens when governments treat AI as a shortcut rather than a tool that still requires human judgement.

This matters far beyond South Africa. Every African country currently drafting AI legislation faces the same temptation and the same risk. South Africa was working toward joining Egypt, Rwanda, Mauritius and Zambia as African states with adopted national AI policies, in line with the African Union's Continental AI Strategy. That regional momentum has not disappeared. But it has gained a warning label. 


**Background: How the Policy Collapsed**

Cabinet approved the draft policy on 25 March 2026 and again at a special sitting on 1 April, before it was published in the Government Gazette on 10 April for a 60 day public comment period. The document proposed an ambitious new governance architecture. It set out a National AI Commission, an AI Ethics Board, an AI Regulatory Authority, an AI Ombudsperson, a National AI Safety Institute, and an AI Insurance Superfund designed to compensate individuals harmed by AI systems where liability is unclear. 


Then the citations fell apart. News24 reported that the 86 page document contained 67 references, and that at least six sources were fictitious, with editors at the South African Journal of Philosophy, AI and Society, and the Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy confirming the cited articles had never appeared in their publications. The scholars credited with foundational research on AI governance had never written the papers attributed to them. 


Malatsi acted quickly. He described the failure as more than a technical issue, one that compromised the integrity and credibility of the draft policy, and confirmed the most plausible explanation was that AI-generated citations had been included without proper verification. He called it an unacceptable lapse and promised consequence management. 


The fallout followed a predictable path. Two officials were placed on precautionary suspension pending a formal investigation, and a seven-member independent expert panel was appointed to review the original draft, recommend revisions and replace the fabricated citations with verified sources. The revised policy remains on track for Cabinet by November 2026, with January 2027 as a fallback timeline. 


**Analysis: Three Failures Inside One Document**

This was not one failure. It was three, stacked on top of each other.

The first was a verification failure. Nobody checked the citations before Cabinet approved the document twice, before it was gazetted, and before it went out for public comment. The episode has reinforced the view among South African legal analysts that human oversight remains indispensable even as government use of AI expands. 


The second was a transparency failure. There is no indication the drafters disclosed that generative AI tools were used in preparing the document. Disclosure would not have made the fabricated citations acceptable, but it would have signalled where extra scrutiny was needed.


The third was an institutional design failure, separate from the citation scandal itself. Members of Parliament's portfolio committee are now questioning whether South Africa needs seven new AI institutions at all, or whether it should strengthen and give clearer mandates to regulators that already exist. A policy that leans on new bureaucracy rather than existing capacity is harder to implement and easier to get wrong, independent of who or what wrote it. 


Put together, these three failures describe a government that moved with ambition but without the internal checks that ambition requires. That combination is common across the continent right now, because the political incentive to be seen regulating AI quickly is real, and the technical capacity to do it carefully is still being built in most African public administrations.


## Challenges and Limitations

Full details of the department's internal review process are not yet public, so this analysis cannot assess exactly how the fabricated citations entered the final draft or who signed off at each stage. The independent panel's specific findings have also not been released. Any recommendations here should be read as structural lessons rather than a verdict on individual officials, since consequence management for named staff is still underway and outside the scope of a policy analysis.

## Opportunities and Recommendations

**For Governments**
Establish a mandatory citation and source verification step before any policy document reaches Cabinet, separate from the drafting team. Require disclosure whenever generative AI tools are used in preparing government documents, at any stage.

**For African Institutions**
The African Union should use this episode to issue continent-wide guidance on AI-assisted policy drafting, given the Continental AI Strategy timeline that South Africa's own draft was designed to follow. A shared verification standard would help every member state avoid repeating this failure.

**For Private Sector**
Legal and compliance teams should continue investing in AI governance readiness even while South Africa lacks a formal framework, since a revised policy is expected and the underlying direction of travel has not changed. 

**For International Partners**
Development partners supporting African AI governance capacity building should fund verification tooling and technical review capacity, not just policy drafting workshops. The gap exposed here was not ambition. It was quality control.

**For Researchers**
Academic institutions, including the journals whose names were misused in this episode, have a role in publicly flagging citation fraud quickly, as happened here. That responsiveness should be documented as a model for other jurisdictions.

## Conclusion

South Africa's AI policy did not fail because its ambitions were wrong. A risk-based framework modelled on international best practice was a reasonable starting point. It failed because nobody verified the evidence base before it reached the public. That is a fixable problem, and South Africa now has a chance to fix it in full view of the rest of the continent.

The lesson for every other African government drafting AI policy right now is not to slow down. It is to build the verification step in from the start, so the next policy scandal does not have to happen for the lesson to land.
